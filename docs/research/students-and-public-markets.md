# Finance Students and Public-Markets Roles: Market Research for YouBank

Prepared 2026-09-20. Scope: (A) students and early-career recruits targeting IB, consulting, PE, VC, equity research, corporate finance and hedge funds; (B) public-markets professionals in sell-side and buy-side equity research, hedge funds (fundamental L/S, event-driven, distressed, multi-manager pods), credit research (loans, HY, IG) and sales & trading. Method: 10 web searches plus roughly 55 direct page reads (about 50 successful) across WSO, Mergers & Inquisitions, 10x EBITDA, Peak Frameworks, BIWS, Wall Street Prep, Training The Street, CFA Institute, Dartmouth and Columbia career centers, SEC and FINRA data pages, hedge-fund practitioner sites, credit-research vendors, and the full text of the BIWS "400 Investment Banking Interview Questions" guide. Every URL in the Sources list was either fetched directly or returned by a search; none were invented. Where a page could not be fetched (WSO forum threads and investor.gov return 403 to automated readers), the fact is attributed to a page that was read, or marked as general industry knowledge.

How this connects to YouBank: the onboarding survey already has a `student` role, the comps engine, SEC company-facts ingestion and the Neon database exist, and the AI copilot layer is live. Parts A6/B7 propose features that mostly reuse those pieces; the top-10 lists are ordered by (value to the user) x (reuse of what exists) / (build cost).

## PART A: Finance students and early-career recruits

### A1. Who the students are and what they want

Segments observed across the sources:

| Segment | Typical profile | Primary goal | Secondary goals |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target/semi-target undergraduate (sophomore) | Business/econ/engineering major, 3.7+ GPA, finance club member | Junior-summer IB, S&T or consulting internship that converts to a full-time offer | Sophomore "early insight"/diversity programs; a finance-adjacent sophomore summer |
| Non-target undergraduate | Same ambitions, no on-campus pipeline | Get any first-round interview via 1,000-2,000 cold emails and boutique internships | Lateral into a bulge bracket or elite boutique after 1-2 years |
| Junior/senior with a summer offer | Returning summer analyst or full-time analyst-to-be | Convert the return offer; prepare for PE on-cycle within 6-9 weeks of starting full-time | Hedge fund, growth equity and ER options |
| MBA (first year) | Career switcher from consulting, engineering, military, Big 4 | Summer associate offer in IB (recruiting starts in the first semester) or consulting (deadlines Nov-Dec) | Post-MBA VC/PE associate seats (rare, network-driven) |
| Public-markets-minded student | Runs a personal portfolio, writes stock pitches, competes in CFA Research Challenge | Point72 Academy, Citadel/Balyasny early-career programs, sell-side ER, long-only AM | CFA Level I before graduation |
| Corporate-finance/FLDP candidate | Accounting or finance major, less deal-oriented | Rotational FP&A/treasury program at a large corporate | Later move into corp dev |

What they say they want (from career-center guidance and recruiting sites): a clear, dated plan; to know "what to learn in what order"; a bank of technical questions with model answers; someone to run mock interviews; help writing a one-page resume and networking emails; a stock pitch they can defend; a way to track hundreds of applications and contacts; and reassurance about timelines that move earlier every year. Point72 Academy alone drew over 30,000 applications for roughly 55 internship seats in its 2023 cycle (about 0.6% acceptance), which illustrates the scale of the funnel these students are competing in.

### A2. Recruiting timelines and processes by track

All dates below are stated relative to today (2026-09-20). The recurring theme in every source is compression: IB junior-summer applications now open roughly 18 months before the internship, PE on-cycle runs within weeks of analysts starting full time, and consulting deadlines cluster in July-September of the year before the internship.

**Investment banking (summer analyst, 2028 cycle for current sophomores; 2027 cycle for current juniors)**

- Sophomore fall (now): early insight, "Spring Insight", "Sophomore Summit" and diversity programs (Bank of America, Goldman Sachs, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley, UBS and others) accept applications; they last one day to two weeks and often feed early superdays or direct conversion to summer internships.
- For the summer-2027 class, Adventis's tracker shows the first postings in August 2025 (PwC, Alvarez & Marsal), a large wave in November-December 2025 (RBC, BMO, Houlihan Lokey, Citadel, then JPMorgan, UBS, Lazard, Barclays, Jefferies), and Goldman Sachs, Evercore, Bain Capital and Ares in January 2026. As of September 1, 2026 the tracker shows 180 firms, 113 posted, 67 still to come, with 22+ expected in September (Piper Sandler, GTCR, Cantor and others). Expect the summer-2028 cycle to repeat this shape one year later: waves in November 2026-January 2027.
- Sophomore spring (January-March) is the peak: a surge of applications, HireVue/Suited/Pymetrics screens, first rounds and superdays run on a rolling basis; 10x EBITDA notes offers frequently arrive within 24 hours of a superday. Middle-market, regional and boutique banks keep posting through the summer and into junior fall, and postings stay open anywhere from two weeks to six months.
- Prerequisite that has hardened: a finance-adjacent sophomore summer (boutique advisory, wealth management, corporate finance, search fund, or a finance club deliverable).
- Full-time: mostly return offers from the summer; accelerated full-time interviews for non-returners run August-October of senior year.

**Private equity (on-cycle and off-cycle)**

- 2026 on-cycle for associates starting in 2028 was expected to launch August-October 2026, i.e., roughly 6-9 weeks after first-year analysts started. Headhunters (CPI, Dynamics Search Partners, SG Partners, Henkel; each fund uses one exclusive firm) build lists in July-August, run intro calls in late August-September, and first call to signed offer can take under 72 hours with exploding deadlines.
- Process: headhunter screen (30 min) -> first round (behavioral plus paper LBO or accretion/dilution) -> modeling test (1-3 hours in Excel, or take-home 24-72 hours producing a memo and model) -> case study with 30-60 minutes of pushback -> partner round.
- What candidates must have ready: a 10-minute paper LBO from memory, a memorized MOIC-to-IRR table for 5-year holds (1.5x is about 8.4%, 2.0x about 15%, 3.0x about 25%, 5.0x about 38%), and two or three deal walk-throughs with a 30-second version and a 3-5 minute "PE lens" version.
- Off-cycle: middle-market, growth and some upper-middle-market funds recruit year-round on 4-8 week timelines and are a growing share of hiring.

**Management consulting (undergraduate and MBA)**

- Undergraduate: networking January-April; MBB applications open June-July; deadlines July-September (PrepLounge lists BCG summer associate deadlines of June 23 and September 3, 2026, and McKinsey's full-time business analyst deadline of August 11, 2026); interviews August-October; offers September-November with 1-2 week acceptance windows. Big 4, Oliver Wyman, Kearney and EY-Parthenon run rolling processes; L.E.K. pre-MBA programs closed June 6, 2026.
- MBA: applications open August-September after orientation; full-time deadlines cluster in September, internship deadlines in November; interviews October-December; offers into January.
- Formats: firm online assessments, then two to three case rounds (interviewer-led at McKinsey, candidate-led at Bain, a mix at BCG) plus a personal-experience/behavioral interview. Bain's own page describes a recruiter call, several rounds, and a case interview. Leland recommends starting prep 9-12 months ahead; Columbia's guidance is that most candidates over-prepare cases and under-prepare behavioral stories.

**Equity research**

- Unstructured and "as needed": large banks run some campus and MBA hiring, but most seats open when an associate leaves. Stages: phone/video first round -> superday -> modeling test or written case. Candidates need two pitches (one buy, one hold/sell) of 60-90 seconds, must avoid stocks the interviewer covers, and are judged on writing and evident passion for markets; industry specialists (biotech, energy) are welcomed as career changers.

**Hedge funds (undergraduate and lateral)**

- Point72 Academy: an 8-week summer internship in the final university summer feeding a 10-month paid Associate program; 2027-cycle applicants must graduate between December 2027 and July 2028; applications open in autumn and are reviewed rolling; four stages (resume screen, word-limited questionnaire, case study plus stock pitch with original models, then multiple 30-minute PM/analyst interviews). Intern base pay for 2027 is $120,000-$140,000 annualized. Over 200 graduates have become Point72 analysts and about 65% of Point72 PMs were developed internally. Citadel and Balyasny run autumn-window early-career programs.
- Laterals from IB/ER: about 80% of hedge-fund hiring is off-cycle; typical move after 2-3 years (12-24 months from restructuring/credit groups); process is 1-2 weeks once it starts; headhunters include Glocap, Dynamics, SearchOne, Amity, Selby Jennings and Sheffield Haworth; multi-managers increasingly recruit through internal talent teams.

**Venture capital**

- Undergraduate analyst seats are rare; pre-MBA associates come from IB, consulting, product or startup operations after 2-3 years; post-MBA associates from top programs are the partner track. Early-stage firms test sourcing, networking and market judgment; growth-stage firms test execution and financial analysis. Hiring is network-driven and continuous rather than seasonal.

**Corporate finance / FLDPs**

- Recruiting happens much closer to the start date than IB; interviews are accounting-heavy (three statements, journal entries, NPV, breakeven, capital structure) and may test Excel, Power BI or Tableau; junior work is five-year plans, variance analysis, reconciliations and cash forecasting.

**Certifications and competitions**

- CFA: four windows (February, May, August, November); Level I in all four, Level II in May/August/November, Level III in February/August. Fees from USD 1,140 (early) to 1,490 (standard) for Levels I-II, 1,240/1,590 for Level III; the one-time enrollment fee was eliminated in February 2026; total program cost USD 3,520-4,570; USD 250 to reschedule. Students can register while still in university (CFA Institute permits candidates within 23 months of graduation; verify on the site).
- CPA: relevant mainly to accounting-track and corporate-finance students; 150 credit hours and state licensure.
- CFA Institute Research Challenge: teams of three to five students write an equity research report on an assigned public company and deliver a ten-minute presentation; local rounds early in the calendar year, sub-regional in March, regional semifinals April 6-7, 2027, regional finals April 8-9, 2027, global final the week of May 10, 2027 in Singapore. Judged on analysis, valuation, report writing and presentation.
- Stock-pitch and case competitions at universities follow the same report-plus-deck format; they are the most common "work sample" that ER and hedge-fund interviewers ask about.

**Top 60 technical interview questions with brief model answers.** Verbatim-style phrasing follows the 400-questions guide, Wall Street Playbook's 100-question list and 10x EBITDA's core technicals guide; answers are condensed.

*Accounting (1-12)*
1. Walk me through the three statements. Income statement runs revenue to net income; balance sheet lists assets, liabilities and equity (assets = liabilities + equity); cash flow statement starts with net income, adjusts for non-cash items and working capital, then investing and financing, ending in net change in cash.
2. How do the statements link? Net income flows to retained earnings and to the top of the cash flow statement; balance-sheet changes appear as working-capital lines; investing and financing flows change PP&E, debt and equity; ending cash from the CFS is the balance-sheet cash.
3. Depreciation rises by $10 (40% tax). Operating income -10, net income -6; CFS: net income -6 plus add-back 10 = cash +4; balance sheet: PP&E -10, cash +4, so assets -6; retained earnings -6; it balances.
4. One statement to judge health? Cash flow statement, because it shows actual cash generation independent of non-cash accounting.
5. Two statements? Income statement and balance sheet, because a cash flow statement can be derived from beginning and ending balance sheets.
6. Accounts receivable vs deferred revenue? AR is revenue recognized but not yet collected (asset); deferred revenue is cash collected for services not yet delivered (liability).
7. Working capital and negative working capital? Operating current assets minus operating current liabilities; negative can indicate an efficient model funded by customers and suppliers (subscriptions, retailers) or, if deteriorating, liquidity stress.
8. Capitalize vs expense? Capitalize when the outlay creates a benefit beyond one year; it moves through D&A over time and raises near-term earnings versus expensing.
9. Deferred tax liability vs asset? DTL arises when book tax expense exceeds cash tax (accelerated tax depreciation); DTA arises from NOLs or expenses recognized for book before tax.
10. What is goodwill? Purchase price minus fair value of net identifiable assets; not amortized for public companies under US GAAP, tested annually for impairment.
11. $100 capex funded by debt. CFS: investing -100, financing +100, no cash change; balance sheet: PP&E +100, debt +100; income statement unchanged until depreciation and interest begin.
12. Inventory rises by $20 paid in cash. CFS: working capital -20; balance sheet: inventory +20, cash -20; no income-statement effect until sold through COGS.

*Enterprise and equity value (13-20)*
13. Define both and the bridge. Equity value = share price x diluted shares; enterprise value = equity value + debt + preferred + non-controlling interests - cash (plus other debt-like items).
14. Why subtract cash? It is a non-operating asset a buyer effectively receives; strictly only excess cash should be subtracted.
15. Why add non-controlling interest? Because consolidated revenue and EBITDA include 100% of the subsidiary, so EV must reflect 100% of its capital for consistency.
16. Can EV be negative? Yes, when cash exceeds market cap plus debt; public equity value cannot be negative.
17. Which metrics pair with which value? EV with revenue, EBITDA, EBIT (available to all capital providers); equity value with net income, book value and levered FCF.
18. Company issues $100 of stock and holds the cash. Equity value +100, cash +100, EV unchanged.
19. Diluted shares. Treasury stock method: in-the-money options exercised, proceeds repurchase shares at the current price; add RSUs; if-converted for convertibles.
20. $100M market cap, $50M debt, $20M cash. EV = $130M.

*Valuation and comps (21-28)*
21. Three major methodologies? Comparable companies, precedent transactions, DCF; also LBO analysis, sum-of-the-parts, liquidation and replacement value.
22. Rank them. No fixed ranking; precedents usually exceed comps because of the control premium; DCF is the most variable.
23. How do you pick comps? Same industry, similar size, growth, margins, geography and business model; 5-10 names; favor forward multiples.
24. Why are precedents higher? Acquirers pay control premiums and share synergies.
25. Why does a company trade at a premium? Faster growth, higher margins or ROIC, durable moat, lower risk, scarcity value.
26. EV/EBITDA vs EV/revenue? Revenue for unprofitable or hyper-growth names; EBITDA when profitable and capital intensity is comparable.
27. How do you value a bank? P/E, price to tangible book, dividend discount or residual income; not EV/EBITDA, since interest is operating for a bank.
28. What is a football field? A chart of valuation ranges per method, usually 25th-75th percentile multiples, used to triangulate rather than average.

*DCF (29-38)*
29. Walk me through a DCF. Project unlevered FCF for 5-10 years, estimate terminal value (perpetuity growth or exit multiple), discount both at WACC, sum to EV, bridge to equity value and divide by diluted shares.
30. Unlevered FCF formula. EBIT x (1 - tax) + D&A - capex - increase in NWC.
31. WACC. E/V x cost of equity + D/V x cost of debt x (1 - t), using market values and target structure.
32. Cost of equity. CAPM: risk-free rate + beta x equity risk premium (plus size or country premia if used).
33. Levered vs unlevered beta. Unlever peer betas with beta_L / (1 + (1 - t) x D/E), take the median, relever at the target structure.
34. Terminal value and sanity check. Gordon growth (g at or below long-run GDP/inflation, roughly 2-3%) versus exit multiple; cross-check the implied multiple or implied growth; TV often 60-80% of EV.
35. Mid-year convention. Cash arrives through the year, so discount at t - 0.5; raises value slightly.
36. WACC up, value? Down; higher discount rate lowers present values. Higher growth raises value.
37. When not to use a DCF? Unstable or negative cash flows (early-stage), and banks or insurers where debt and working capital are operating items.
38. Levered DCF? Discount levered FCF (after interest and net borrowing) at the cost of equity to get equity value directly.

*Merger model (39-46)*
39. Walk me through a merger model. Set price and consideration mix; project both income statements; combine; adjust for foregone interest on cash and new interest on debt; apply the buyer's tax rate; divide by the new share count; compare pro forma EPS to standalone.
40. Rule of thumb for accretion. All-stock: accretive if the buyer's P/E exceeds the seller's; cash or debt: accretive if the seller's earnings yield exceeds the after-tax cost of the cash or debt used.
41. Why acquire? Market share, faster growth, undervaluation, customer access, technology, synergies.
42. Synergies and their value. Revenue (less reliable) and cost (more reliable); value as NPV of after-tax synergies, or capitalize run-rate synergies at a multiple net of integration cost.
43. Purchase accounting. Write assets to fair value, create goodwill for the excess, add D&A on write-ups and a deferred tax liability in stock deals.
44. Asset vs stock deal. Asset deals (and 338(h)(10) elections) give the buyer a stepped-up tax basis; sellers of C-corps face double taxation in asset deals, so price and structure trade off.
45. Cheapest to most expensive financing. Cash (foregone interest, lowest), debt (after-tax interest), stock (cost of equity, highest).
46. Is accretive always good? No; accretion can be mechanical when buying lower-P/E targets and says nothing about overpayment or value creation.

*LBO (47-54)*
47. Walk me through an LBO. Assumptions (price, leverage, rates, operating case); sources & uses; adjust the balance sheet for new debt, equity and goodwill; project three statements and the debt paydown; exit at an EBITDA multiple; compute IRR and MOIC.
48. Why does leverage boost returns? Less sponsor equity funds the same asset, debt is repaid with the company's cash flow, and interest is tax-deductible.
49. Ideal LBO candidate. Stable cash flows, low capex and working-capital needs, strong management, hard assets or contracted revenue, low existing leverage, operational upside, reasonable entry price.
50. Paper LBO. Example: $100 EBITDA at 10x = $1,000 EV, 5x debt ($500) and $500 equity; EBITDA grows to $150 by year 5, exit at 10x = $1,500; $200 of debt repaid leaves $300; equity = $1,200 = 2.4x, roughly 19% IRR. Memorize the 5-year table: 2.0x is about 15%, 2.5x about 20%, 3.0x about 25%.
51. Sources & uses. Uses: equity purchase price, refinanced debt, fees, minimum cash; sources: revolver, term loans, notes, mezzanine, sponsor equity, rollover.
52. Debt tranches. Revolver, term loan A/B (floating, often cov-lite), senior and subordinated notes (fixed, incurrence covenants), mezzanine/PIK; seniority and pricing rise together.
53. IRR vs MOIC. IRR is time-weighted; MOIC is absolute; both are shown because a fast 2x can beat a slow 3x on IRR.
54. Value-creation levers. EBITDA growth, multiple expansion, deleveraging; dividend recaps return capital early.

*Markets, pitching and brain teasers (55-60)*
55. Pitch me a stock (60-90 seconds). Recommendation with target and horizon; two-line background; two or three thesis points showing what the market misses; dated catalysts; valuation with base/bull/bear; key risks and mitigants.
56. Where are the 10-year Treasury, Fed funds and S&P 500 right now? Know the current levels and the last move; the interviewer is testing habit, not precision.
57. Tell me about a recent deal. Parties, price and multiple, rationale, financing, and your view of whether it creates value.
58. Angle between clock hands at 1:45? 142.5 degrees (120 degrees for four hour-marks plus 22.5 degrees of hour-hand drift).
59. A car drives 60 miles at 30 mph; how fast to average 60 mph over the same 60 miles? Impossible; the two hours are already used.
60. How would you value a company with no revenue? Comparable companies on users or TAM, precedent deals, VC-method backward from exit, scenario DCF; state the assumptions explicitly.

### A6. Feature proposals for students (28 features; top 10 first)

**Top 10 (prioritized)**
1. **Technical Drill Engine** - Adaptive question bank (500+ questions across accounting, EV, valuation, DCF, merger, LBO, credit, markets). Inputs: track, target date, self-rated level. Outputs: daily sets, graded free-text answers, weak-topic map. AI: grades against model answers, generates variants with new numbers, explains mistakes. Difficulty: Medium.
2. **Mock Interviewer** - Live technical, fit and deal-discussion interviews with pushback. Inputs: resume, target firm, mode (phone screen, superday). Outputs: transcript, rubric scores, three fixes. AI: role-plays interviewer, escalates follow-ups, scores structure and accuracy. Difficulty: Medium-High.
3. **Recruiting Calendar and Tracker** - Firm-by-firm posting dates, deadlines, application status and superday outcomes. Inputs: class year, tracks, firm list. Outputs: calendar, alerts, pipeline board, weekly summary. AI: parses posting pages and emails into events, drafts status notes. Difficulty: Medium (data maintenance is the cost).
4. **Stock Pitch Builder** - Guided pitch (recommendation, background, thesis, catalysts, valuation, risks) built on YouBank company data and the comps engine. Inputs: ticker, direction, horizon. Outputs: 2-3 page memo, 90-second script, base/bull/bear table. AI: drafts thesis candidates from filings and transcripts, challenges each claim, produces the consensus-vs-you table. Difficulty: Medium (reuses comps and SEC data).
5. **Paper LBO and Timed Model Tests** - Randomized paper LBOs and 1-3 hour Excel cases with answer keys. Inputs: difficulty, time limit. Outputs: score, step-by-step solution, IRR/MOIC check. AI: generates cases, checks uploaded workbooks cell by cell. Difficulty: Medium-High.
6. **Model Auditor** - Upload an Excel model; get balance, sign, hard-code, circularity and formula-consistency checks. Inputs: .xlsx. Outputs: annotated issue list, fixed-version suggestions. AI: interprets model structure and explains each issue. Difficulty: High (parsing arbitrary workbooks).

## PART B: Public-markets roles

### B1. Role overviews by track

**Sell-side equity research.** Teams of one senior analyst plus one to three associates cover a fixed universe (often 10-25 stocks within a sub-sector). Output is published research: initiations (20-100+ pages), earnings previews and recaps, rating and price-target changes, industry primers and thematic notes; every covered name needs a note each quarter even when there is little to say. Analysts are paid on Institutional Investor and similar rankings, the performance of calls and revenue indirectly generated through trading and corporate access; top-ranked analysts historically clear $1-2.5 million. Associates earn about $125K-$200K, VPs $200K-$300K, directors $300K-$600K and MDs $500K-$1M, mostly base. Hours: about 12 per weekday (55-60 a week), rising to 70-80 hours and 16-hour days in earnings season and conference weeks.

**Buy-side fundamental research (long-only and single-manager L/S).** Analysts cover broader sectors but choose what to dig into, produce internal memos and models that support position recommendations, and advance only by making money. A Yale SOM day-in-the-life shows the rhythm: 7:00 papers, 7:30 sell-side morning-call voicemails, 8:00 investment meeting, earnings calls, management and industry calls, model updates with an associate, recommendation summaries and a deck for PMs by evening. Compensation: analysts $200K-$600K, senior analysts $500K-$1M, PMs $500K-$3M+, largely bonus.

**Multi-manager platforms (pods).** Hundreds of semi-autonomous teams (Millennium 330+ pods, roughly $79B AUM; Point72 185+ teams, about $33.9B), each with a PM, one to three analysts and a defined sector universe, allocated capital by a central CIO office on Sharpe, drawdown and correlation. Work is catalyst-driven: quarterly earnings models, positioning and sentiment, alternative data, expert calls, and pair trades that keep the book beta-neutral. Compensation is a mechanical share of pod P&L (PM 10-20%, analysts $150-200K base plus performance bonus; a strong analyst on a profitable pod can clear $400K-$700K). 15-20% of PMs turn over each year.

**Event-driven and special situations.** Merger arbitrage, spin-offs, activism and restructuring; needs hard catalysts, deal expertise (merger models, SOTP), regulatory timelines and probability-weighted spreads; concentrated books of 10-15 positions.

**Distressed and credit hedge funds.** Long/short credit (HY, loans, structured, derivatives), relative value across the capital structure, covenant analysis (restricted payments baskets, change of control), recovery scenarios, fulcrum-security analysis and restructuring influence; skills are bond math, bankruptcy process and document interpretation. Backgrounds: restructuring IB, leveraged finance, turnaround consulting, law.

**Credit research (sell-side, buy-side, agencies).** Analysts cover 1-2 industries and model each issuer's bonds and loans separately, emphasizing downside: leverage and coverage ratios, liquidity runway, refinancing risk, yield to worst, recovery, covenant packages and relative value versus similar issues. Sell-side fixed-income research associates earn $100K-$150K with 50-55 hour weeks; buy-side analysts $300K+. Vendors such as LevFin Insights (CreditSights) supply new-issue previews, covenant reviews and bankruptcy alerts.

**Sales & trading.** Desks organized by product and client type (G10 rates sales, hedge-fund credit sales, equity derivatives, distressed); salespeople act as agents for client flow, traders price and warehouse risk, structurers design bespoke products, and "flow trading" mixes client coverage with risk-taking. Days start before the open with overnight news and the morning meeting and end with client entertainment; interviews test product knowledge, market views, mental math, programming and brainteasers more than IB interviews do.

### B4. Core methodologies

**Earnings models.** Quarterly, driver-based models: revenue builds by segment (units x price, subscribers x ARPU, same-store sales plus footprint), gross margin and opex lines, non-GAAP reconciliation, EPS and FCF, balance-sheet and buyback assumptions; a "what changed" tab that logs each estimate revision; and a variance tab that compares own estimates to consensus and to the whisper. Peak Frameworks' hedge-fund curriculum describes exactly this: read releases, spread non-GAAP financials, forecast quarterly EPS.

**Price-target frameworks (sell-side).** Target = forward metric (12-24 months out) x target multiple, with the multiple justified by peers, history and growth/margin profile; DCF as a cross-check; sum-of-the-parts for conglomerates; probability-weighted scenarios for binary events. Ratings map to three tiers (Buy/Outperform/Overweight; Hold/Neutral/Market-weight; Sell/Underperform/Underweight), usually defined by expected return relative to a sector or index over 12 months. M&I notes that sell-side reports give a single target rather than a distribution and treat thesis and catalysts more loosely than buy-side memos.

**Thesis structure and variant perception (buy-side).** Six parts: the call (direction and magnitude), a falsifiable thesis, the variant view naming the specific consensus assumption that is wrong, a datable catalyst within 6-12 months, two or three weighted risks, and sizing with a cut level. A worked example uses a 3-to-1 skew: +40% target versus a -12% cut. Interviewers grade reasoning, resilience under pushback and risk discipline ahead of arithmetic. Strong shorts need a near-term catalyst, a variant view below consensus, asymmetric payoff and attention to crowding and borrow cost; valuation alone is the weakest short thesis.

**Catalyst calendars.** Per position: earnings dates, investor days, product launches, regulatory decisions, index rebalances, lock-up expiries, debt maturities, court dates; per book: a weekly view of expected moves (from options) against position sizes to keep event risk within the drawdown budget.

**Position sizing and factor exposure.** Size to the risk budget, not conviction: contribution to book volatility, liquidity (days to exit at a share of ADV), and correlation with existing positions; pairs or sector baskets to neutralize beta and sector; monitor style factors (momentum, value, size, quality, crowding) so the residual is stock-specific; net exposure within roughly plus or minus 20% and gross 4-8x at platforms; explicit stop or reassessment levels. Performance is judged on Sharpe and drawdown behavior, and capital is reallocated by the CIO office on those metrics.

**Credit relative value.** Compare yield to worst and spread per turn of leverage across similar issuers and across an issuer's own capital structure; adjust for seniority, security, covenant quality, call protection, duration and liquidity; build downside cases (EBITDA decline, refinancing at higher rates) and recovery by tranche from an asset-value or going-concern waterfall; express views as overweight/underweight, loan-vs-bond, or basis trades.

**Capital-structure arbitrage and distressed.** Long one instrument and short another of the same issuer (senior loan vs unsecured bond, bond vs CDS, debt vs equity) when relative prices misjudge recovery or timing; in distressed, identify the fulcrum security (the tranche that converts to equity), model Chapter 11 versus liquidation outcomes, and price the security at the discount that clears distressed thresholds (secured in the low 90s or below, unsecured at 60-70 or below).

**Merger arbitrage.** Spread = offer value minus current price; annualize over expected close; weigh regulatory, financing and shareholder-vote risk; downside is the pre-announcement price; hedge stock consideration by shorting the acquirer.

### B6. Glossary (36 terms)

- **Alpha / beta**: Return not explained by the market versus sensitivity to it; pods target beta near zero.
- **Alternative data**: Non-traditional datasets (card spend, web traffic, app downloads, geolocation) used to nowcast KPIs.
- **Basis trade**: Long cash bond vs short CDS (or the reverse) to capture pricing differences.
- **Capital structure**: The ranked stack of an issuer's claims: revolver, term loans, secured notes, unsecured notes, subordinated, preferred, equity.
- **Catalyst (hard vs soft)**: A dated event that forces repricing (earnings, ruling, deal close) versus a plausible but undated development.
- **Channel check**: Primary research with customers, suppliers, distributors or store visits to verify demand.
- **Consensus**: Aggregated sell-side estimates; the buy-side "whisper" is the informal expectation that may differ.
- **Covenant (maintenance vs incurrence)**: Tested regularly on loans versus tested only on actions (new debt, dividends) on bonds; cov-lite loans lack maintenance tests.
- **Crowding**: Many funds in the same position, raising the risk of sharp unwinds; measured with 13F and short-interest data.
- **Days to cover**: Short interest divided by average daily volume.
- **Drawdown limit**: Loss from peak that triggers capital cuts or termination in a pod.
- **Earnings quality**: How closely earnings track cash flow; rising income with flat operating cash flow is a warning.
- **Expected move**: Implied earnings-day move from options, used to size event risk.
- **Factor exposure**: Sensitivity to style factors (value, momentum, size, quality) that must be hedged to isolate stock selection.
- **Fulcrum security**: The tranche in a restructuring that receives the equity because value runs out there.
- **Gross / net exposure**: Long plus short versus long minus short, as a share of capital.
- **Guidance**: Management's forward outlook; the delta versus prior guidance drives the recap note.
- **Initiation of coverage**: The first, longest report on a company (20-100+ pages) with industry work, model and target.
- **Institutional Investor (II) ranking**: Buy-side vote that ranks sell-side analysts and drives their pay.
- **Merger arbitrage spread**: Offer value minus current price, annualized over expected time to close.
- **Morning meeting / morning call**: Daily pre-open session where research presents changes to sales and trading.
- **Non-GAAP reconciliation**: Adjustments from reported to adjusted earnings; analysts "spread" these into the model.
- **Pair trade**: Long one name and short a peer to neutralize market and sector risk.
- **Position sizing**: Setting the weight from risk budget, volatility, liquidity and conviction, with a defined cut level.
- **Price target**: A 12-month value from a target multiple or DCF that anchors the rating.
- **Recovery rate**: Expected repayment on a claim in default, by tranche.
- **Relative value (credit)**: Comparing spread and yield per unit of risk across similar issues or within a capital structure.
- **Sharpe ratio**: Excess return per unit of volatility; the primary pod performance metric.
- **Short interest**: Open short positions on a settlement date, reported twice monthly to FINRA.
- **Sum-of-the-parts**: Valuing each segment separately and summing, common in spin-off and conglomerate work.
- **Spread per turn of leverage**: Credit spread divided by debt/EBITDA, a quick relative-value ratio.
- **TRACE**: FINRA's reporting engine for OTC bond trades, reported within 15 minutes for corporates.
- **Variant perception**: A specific, defensible view that differs from what the market has priced.
- **Waterfall**: Order in which recoveries are distributed across the capital structure.
- **Whisper number**: The unofficial expectation among investors that a company must beat to trade up.
- **Yield to worst**: The lowest yield across possible call and maturity dates.

27. **Covenant Extractor** - Restricted-payments baskets, incremental capacity, change-of-control and J.Crew/Serta-style blocker language from documents. High.
28. **Distressed Scenario Modeler** - Chapter 11 vs liquidation outcomes with fulcrum identification and recovery ranges. High.
29. **Merger-Arb Spread Tracker** - Deals, spreads, annualized returns, regulatory milestones and break-price estimates. Medium.
30. **Sum-of-the-Parts Builder** - Segment valuations with peer multiples from the comps engine. Low-Medium (reuses comps).
31. **Client Question Responder (sell-side)** - Drafts answers to investor questions from the model and published notes, with compliance-friendly citations. Medium.
32. **S&T Morning Pack** - Overnight moves, axes, economic calendar and talking points per desk; brainteaser and market-knowledge drills for juniors. Low.
